[0001] [TITLE] Targeting the PINK1/Parkin-FNDC5 pathway: a novel mechanism of icariin in regulating muscle-bone metabolic coupling in osteosarcopenia

[0002] [ABSTRACT] Background

[0003] [ABSTRACT] Osteosarcopenia (OS) significantly elevates fracture risk in the elderly due to concurrent skeletal and muscular metabolic decline. Mitochondrial autophagy dysregulation is a significant mechanism driving myoskeletal metabolic imbalance, and myofibroblast-derived factor 5 (FNDC5)/irisin is a crucial mediator in the myoskeletal dialogue. It is unclear if the medicinal ingredient icariin (ICA) from the traditional Chinese herb Epimedium can work in concert to prevent and treat OS through this pathway, despite research suggesting that exercise may enhance muscular performance by modulating the FNDC5–PINK1/Parkin pathway.

[0004] [ABSTRACT] Methods

[0005] [ABSTRACT] With the use of dexamethasone (DEX) and ovariectomy (OVX), a female SD rat OS model was created. Low, medium, and high dosages (40, 80, and 120 mg/kg/d) of ICA were then administered. Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT), forelimb grip strength tests, and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) were used to evaluate bone microarchitecture, muscle performance, and body composition, respectively. Using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining, the morphology of muscle tissue was investigated. In skeletal muscle, Western blotting revealed the expression of FNDC5 and mitochondrial autophagy-related proteins (PINK1, Parkin, LC3, p62). ELISA was used to evaluate the serum levels of FNDC5, muscle growth inhibitor (GDF-8), and inflammatory markers interleukin-6 (IL-6) and C-reactive protein (CRP). To directly verify the causal role of the PINK1/Parkin pathway in mediating ICA’s effects, an in vitro experiment using the mitochondrial autophagy inhibitor Mdivi-1 was performed alongside the CCCP-induced mitophagy model in C2C12 myoblasts. We used Bayesian co-localization, HEIDI heterogeneity testing, and summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) to further integrate expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) data with genome-wide association study (GWAS) data from eQTLGen and IEU Consortium in order to genetically validate the causal relationship between FNDC5 and OS.

[0006] [ABSTRACT] Results

[0007] [ABSTRACT] DEX + OVX effectively produced a classic OS phenotype in rats, which was marked by a marked increase in fat percentage (FAT) (p < 0.01) and a significant decrease in bone mineral density (whole body, femur, spine), bone mineral content (BMC), muscle mass, muscle mass index (SMI), and grip strength (p < 0.01). These alterations were significantly reversed by ICA intervention, especially in the medium and high-dose groups. It increased bone mineral density and BMC (p < 0.001), improved bone microstructural parameters (increased BV/TV, Tb.Th, and Tb.N; decreased Tb.Sp), enhanced muscle mass and SMI, enlarged muscle fiber cross-sectional area, and reduced fibrosis and fat infiltration. ICA also significantly reduced serum GDF-8, CRP, and IL-6 levels (p < 0.0001) while increasing FNDC5 concentration (p < 0.0001). Mechanistic studies revealed that ICA suppressed excessive mitochondrial autophagy in muscle tissue (downregulating PINK1 and Parkin proteins) while upregulating FNDC5 expression (p < 0.0001) and promoting the expression of osteogenic-related proteins Runx2 and OCN. Additionally, in vitro tests demonstrated that ICA (300 µM) reversed the inhibition of FNDC5 expression (p < 0.01), prevented excessive activation of the PINK1/Parkin pathway (p < 0.01), and mitigated the drop in mitochondrial membrane potential caused by CCCP. Crucially, Mdivi-1 suppression of the PINK1/Parkin pathway replicated the effects of ICA, resulting in elevated expression of FNDC5. Additionally, co-treatment with ICA and Mdivi-1 did not result in an additive impact on FNDC5 overexpression, suggesting that ICA modulates FNDC5 via the PINK1/Parkin pathway. Its preventive causative function was supported by SMR analysis, which showed that genetically predicted greater FNDC5 levels were strongly linked with decreased OS risk.

[0008] [ABSTRACT] Conclusions

[0009] [ABSTRACT] This study suggests that ICA improves OS in association with inhibition of pathological mitochondrial autophagy and modulation of the PINK1/Parkin–FNDC5 pathway, providing a basis for further mechanistic studies. Genetic evidence further supports FNDC5 as a potential therapeutic target for OS. These findings provide preclinical evidence for ICA’s potential in postmenopausal osteosarcopenia, though applicability to age-related, male, or disuse-related forms requires validation.

[0012] [INTRO] Introduction

[0013] [INTRO] Osteosarcopenia (OS) is a major public health issue increasingly prominent with population aging, characterized by the coexistence of progressive decline in skeletal muscle mass and function alongside reduced bone mineral density and impaired bone microarchitecture. This complex “musculoskeletal dialogue” mechanism malfunction is the cause of the musculoskeletal system’s comorbidity rather than an isolated event. In the end, it results in a sharp rise in the risk of falls for patients, a notable increase in the frequency of fractures, a sharp reduction in quality of life, and elevated mortality rates, placing a tremendous strain on society and healthcare systems. Thus, there is great scientific and clinical benefit in investigating the pathophysiological underpinnings of OS and investigating synergistic treatment approaches that target bone and muscle metabolism at the same time.

[0014] [INTRO] Skeletal muscle-derived factors, or myokines, serve a critical mediating function in the closely connected network between muscle and bone that sustains metabolic homeostasis. One such myokine that promotes bone growth, improves metabolism, and lowers inflammation to preserve muscle-bone homeostasis is irisin, a cleavage product of FNDC5. The metabolic decoupling in OS is believed to be caused by dysregulation of the FNDC5/irisin axis. Notably, recent studies have shown that resistance training can improve muscle function and mitochondrial integrity by activating the irisin/FNDC5–PINK1/Parkin–LC3/P62 pathway. This highlights the potential of targeting this pathway for musculoskeletal health.

[0015] [INTRO] A rising amount of research in recent years has suggested that sarcopenia and osteoporosis share a shared early pathophysiological origin in mitochondrial dysfunction. Mitophagy, as the core quality control system for selectively eliminating damaged mitochondria, is crucial for maintaining energy homeostasis and survival in myoblasts. Damaged mitochondria build up when mitochondrial autophagy is compromised (either overactivated or insufficient), which eventually causes muscle atrophy and bone metabolic disorders by causing reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts, insufficient ATP production, increased apoptosis, and inflammatory cascades. Particularly in OS, how aberrant skeletal muscle mitochondrial autophagy activation impacts FNDC5 expression and function, thereby disrupting the muscle-bone dialogue, remains an urgent scientific question to be explored.

[0016] [INTRO] Significant anti-osteoporosis benefits and improved muscular function are demonstrated by ICA, the main active ingredient in the traditional Chinese medication Epimedium. While it is a multi-target agent, its precise mechanism in restoring the muscle-bone metabolic axis, particularly through the regulation of skeletal muscle mitophagy and FNDC5 expression, is not fully understood. Building upon the known exercise-induced pathway, this study hypothesizes that ICA may exert its musculoskeletal protective effects by similarly targeting the PINK1/Parkin–FNDC5 axis.

[0017] [INTRO] SMR analysis, as a powerful tool that utilizes genetic variation as an instrumental variable to infer causal relationships between exposure and outcomes, has been widely applied in studies of osteoporosis and sarcopenia. However, the SMR approach has not yet been used to systematically confirm the causal link between FNDC5 and OS.

[0018] [INTRO] Thus, the goals of this research are to: (1) create a rat OS model to assess the therapeutic effects of ICA; (2) examine whether ICA improves musculoskeletal metabolism and rescues FNDC5 expression in association with modifying the PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy pathway; (3) investigate the regulatory effects of ICA on this axis in vitro; and (4) use SMR analysis to provide genetic causal evidence supporting FNDC5 as a therapeutic target for OS. This study intends to extend the paradigm beyond exercise-mediated protection by providing new insights into OS etiology and suggesting a potential pharmaceutical approach that targets the PINK1/Parkin–FNDC5 pathway.

[0022] [METHODS] In vivo experiments

[0023] [METHODS] This research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Approval No.: 2403667). SPF-grade female SD rats (weight 200 ± 20 g). They were bought from Guizhou Baite Trade Co., Ltd. and were between 8 and 12 weeks old. Every rat was kept in a specified pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility with regulated circumstances, including a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle, a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, and a relative humidity of 50 ± 10%. Water and standard rodent food were freely available. The rats were randomly divided into five groups (10 rats per group): Control group, Model group, low-dose icariin group (40 mg/kg, LD group), medium-dose icariin group (80 mg/kg, MD group), and high-dose icariin group (120 mg/kg, HD group). The modeling procedure is as follows: After anesthesia, rats underwent bilateral ovariectomy in all groups except the control group, which only underwent bilateral removal of an equal volume of dorsal adipose tissue. The model group and all drug-administered groups received intraperitoneal injections of sodium phosphate dexamethasone (1 mg/kg, dissolved in physiological saline) twice weekly postoperatively. The control group received equivalent volumes of physiological saline during the same period, continuing for 8 weeks. ICA (purity ≥ 98%, Xi’an Wanzhi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was given by gavage every day for 12 weeks while suspended in physiological saline. As a vehicle control, the control and model groups were given the same amount of physiological saline via gavage throughout the same time period. The known efficacy and safety profiles documented in earlier preclinical investigations on bone metabolism and muscle function served as the main basis for the selection of in vivo ICA dosages (40, 80, and 120 mg/kg/day). In mouse models of osteoporosis and sarcopenia, these dosages have demonstrated notable osteoprotective and myoprotective benefits without causing discernible toxicity when administered over an extended period of time. This range allows for the examination of potential dose-dependent effects of ICA in our OS model. According to body surface area conversion, the high dose (120 mg/kg) in rats corresponds to a human equivalent dose of approximately 19.2 mg/kg, or ~ 1344 mg/day for a 70-kg adult. This exceeds doses used in clinical trials of icariin (typically 60–300 mg/day) by > 4–20 fold. Therefore, the translational relevance of these doses is uncertain without pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic validation. See Figure S1 (Supplementary Materials) for the animal modeling schematic, and Table 1 for the experimental groups and drug intervention protocols.

[0026] [METHODS] In vitro experiments

[0027] [METHODS] C2C12 mouse myoblasts (Catalog No. CL-0045, Wuhan Punoise Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were exposed to 10 µM CCCP for 12 h in order to create a model of mitochondrial autophagy. To investigate the causal role of the PINK1/Parkin pathway, a separate set of experiments was designed with five groups: (1) Control (C2C12); (2) Model (C2C12 + CCCP); (3) ICA treatment (C2C12 + CCCP + ICA); (4) Inhibitor (C2C12 + CCCP + Mdivi-1); (5) Inhibitor + ICA (C2C12 + CCCP + Mdivi-1 + ICA). A preliminary cell viability assay (CCK-8) was used to determine the ICA concentrations (100, 200, and 300 µM) for in vitro investigations. With 300 µM exhibiting the strongest protective effect, this range included doses that demonstrated minimal cytotoxicity and successfully stimulated C2C12 myoblast proliferation under CCCP-induced stress conditions (Supplementary Materials Figure S2).

[0028] [METHODS] Muscle strength test

[0029] [METHODS] Measure the grip strength of rats’ forelimbs using the YLS-13 A rat grip strength meter (Beijing Lai’ai Technology Development Co., Ltd.). Before testing, allow the rats to adapt to the grip strength meter for 10 min. Then grasp the rat’s tail and, once the rat’s forelimbs are firmly gripped, pull backward smoothly and record the maximum grip strength reading on the instrument. Measure three times and record the results, using the average of the three grip strength readings as an indicator of forelimb muscle strength.

[0030] [METHODS] Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA)

[0031] [METHODS] Use a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scanner to measure fat content, bone mineral content, and total body muscle mass in rats, while also measuring bone mineral density (BMD, g/cm²) in the spine, femur, and total body. Simultaneously calculate the skeletal muscle mass index (SMI, total lean body mass/body weight), bone mineral content percentage (BMC, bone mineral content/body weight), and fat content percentage (FAT, fat content/body weight) of rats.

[0032] [METHODS] Micro-CT

[0033] [METHODS] Using Micro-CT (Ping Sheng Medical Technology, NMC-200) to scan the distal femur, scanning parameters: radial field of view (FOV) 100 mm, maximum axial scanning range 250 mm, scanning speed up to 4 s/bed, reconstruction pixel size min. 2 μm, spatial resolution < 7.5 μm @ 10% MTF. The original image is then reconstructed using the 3D reconstruction software Recon. Then, use the data analysis software Avatar to analyze the region of interest (ROI). By setting uniform parameters, the software calculates bone volume (BV), tissue volume (TV), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), connectivity density (Conn.D), bone mineral density (BMD), and other related measurements.

[0034] [METHODS] HE staining

[0035] [METHODS] For HE staining of the right gastrocnemius and soleus muscle tissues, first stain the paraffin-embedded sections with hematoxylin for 5 to 10 min to stain the cell nuclei. After staining, rinse the sections with running water to remove excess dye, then wash with deionized water. Counterstain the cytoplasm with eosin, adjusting the staining time between 30 s and 3 min as needed. After re-staining, dehydrate the sections by continuously immersing them in 70%, 80%, and 90% ethanol, and finally in anhydrous ethanol, for 10 s each. Then, clear the sections twice with xylene for 5 min each time. Mount the sections with neutral resin. The staining results were evaluated under a microscope, showing blue-stained cell nuclei and cytoplasm ranging from pink to red. In addition, photographs of areas with clear and complete tissue structures were taken for further analysis, and the size of muscle fibers was quantified using ImageJ software.

[0036] [METHODS] Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

[0037] [METHODS] In this study, ELISA was used to quantitatively analyze serum inflammatory, muscle-related factors, and FNDC5 in SD rats. The Rat C-Reactive Protein (CRP) ELISA Kit (BY-ER330078) was used to measure plasma levels of CRP. The kit has a minimum detection limit of 10 ng/mL and a detection range of 75 to 2400 ng/mL. Rat Interleukin-6 (IL-6) ELISA Kit (BY-ER330219) is used to measure IL-6 plasma levels. The kit has a minimum detection limit of 1.0 ng/mL and a detection range of 5 to 160 pg/mL. Rat Growth Differentiation Factor 8 (GDF-8) ELISA Kit (BY-ER334915) is used to measure plasma levels of GDF-8. The kit has a minimum detection limit of 0.1 ng/mL and a detection range of 2.5 to 80 ng/mL. Rat Fibronectin Domain-Containing Protein 5 (FNDC5) ELISA Kit (BY-ER3200127) is used to measure plasma levels of FNDC5. The kit has a minimum detection limit of 0.1 ng/mL and a detection range of 3.125 to 100 ng/mL. All procedures are strictly conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

[0038] [METHODS] CCK-8 method

[0039] [METHODS] Seed C2C12 cells at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well into a 96-well plate, with 100 µL per well, and incubate overnight. In the culture plates, except for the control group, add 100 µL of 10 µM mitochondrial autophagy activator CCCP to each well, and incubate in the incubator for 12 h. After 12 h, the treatment group was exposed to different concentrations (100 µM, 200 µM, 300 µM) of ICA for 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h. After each exposure period, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well under dark conditions, and the absorbance (OD) at 450 nm was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) Microplate reader.

[0040] [METHODS] Mitochondrial membrane potential detection

[0041] [METHODS] After the cells reach the logarithmic growth phase, prepare a single-cell suspension, adjust the cell count to an appropriate level, and inoculate 5 × 105 cells per well into a 6-well plate, 2 mL/well, and culture overnight. In the culture plates, except for the control group, 1 mL of 10 µM mitochondrial autophagy activator CCCP was added to each well and incubated in a culture incubator for 12 h. After 12 h, the treatment group was added to 300 µM ICA for 24 h. After completion, the culture medium was removed and washed once with PBS. Add 0.5 mL of culture medium and 0.5 mL of freshly prepared JC-1 staining working solution, mix the two, and incubate at 37 °C for 20 min. After completion, aspirate the supernatant, wash twice with freshly prepared JC-1 staining buffer (1X), resuspend the cells in an appropriate amount of JC-1 staining buffer (1X), and analyze using a flow cytometer.

[0042] [METHODS] Protein blot analysis

[0043] [METHODS] First, extract proteins from cells and muscle tissue, and determine protein concentration using the BCA protein assay kit (cwbiotech 02912E). Separate equal amounts of denatured proteins using 8% or 10% polyacrylamide gel via SDS-PAGE. Subsequently, transfer the separated proteins to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. Then, block the membrane with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 2 h to prevent non-specific binding. After closing, incubate the membrane with anti-PINK1 (1:1000, Bioss, BS-22173R), Parkin (1:1000, Bioss, BS-23687R), LC3 (1:500, Solarbio, K008014P), p62 (1:1000, Bioss, BS-55207R), FNDC5 (1:1000, Bioss, BS-8486R), Runx2 (1:1000, BS-1134R), OCN (1:500, BS-4917R), Actin (1:20,000, Bioss, BS-10966R) as the internal control. On the second day, wash the membrane three times with TBST, then incubate with the appropriate secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. Finally, detect the protein using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Millipore WBKLS0500) and observe the protein bands by exposing the membrane. Quantify gel and Western blot results using ImageJ software.

[0047] [METHODS] Mendelian randomization analysis

[0048] [METHODS] The IEU OpenGWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) provided the GWAS pooled data for OS used in this investigation. The FNDC5 gene’s expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) data and OS GWAS data were combined using the TwoSampleMR software program (v0.5.7). Instrumental variables (IVs) were selected as SNPs associated with FNDC5 expression at genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10⁻⁸) in muscle eQTL data from the eQTLGen consortium. Independent SNPs were obtained by LD clumping (r² < 0.001, window = 10,000 kb). The F-statistic for each SNP was calculated to ensure instrument strength (F > 10). GWAS summary data for OS-related traits were obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS database (including UK Biobank and GEnuINE cohorts). Lastly, SNPs that achieved genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10⁻⁸) were kept for further examination. We utilized five MR estimation techniques to evaluate the causal relationship between FNDC5 gene expression and OS risk; the Wald ratio approach was applied when only one SNP was available for inference.

[0049] [METHODS] Bayesian co-localization analysis

[0050] [METHODS] We also used Bayesian colocalization analysis (coloc) to determine if the causative genetic variation shared by OS GWAS signals and FNDC5 eQTL signals is the same. The posterior probabilities were computed using a 100-kilobase area surrounding the index SNP, combining the pooled eQTL results for FNDC5 with the GWAS statistics for OS. While hypothesis H4 proposed the existence of common causative variations, hypothesis H3 proposed that the two qualities were caused by different causal variants. SNP.PP.H4 > 0.95 was chosen as the significance level in order to create strong colocalization connections.

[0051] [METHODS] Statistical analysis

[0052] [METHODS] Performing SMR using R programming (version 4.3.3), GraphPad Prism 10.3.1, and ImageJ were used to analyze the results of this study. All continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical differences between groups were assessed using Tukey’s post hoc test following one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

[0053] [RESULTS] Results

[0054] [RESULTS] ICA significantly improves body composition and bone microarchitecture degeneration in DEX + OVX rats

[0055] [RESULTS] Following DEX + OVX intervention, DXA measurements revealed that compared with the Control group, rats in the Model group exhibited significantly reduced whole-body bone mineral density, femoral bone mineral density, spinal bone mineral density, bone mineral content, and Total body muscle mass (p < 0.01; Fig. 1A–E). Simultaneously, fat content significantly increased (p < 0.0001; Fig. 1F), indicating that the model group experienced severe bone loss and fat accumulation. Further analysis revealed that both SMI and BMC were significantly reduced in the Model group compared to the Control group (p < 0.001; Fig. 1G–H), while FAT was significantly elevated (p < 0.001; Fig. 1I). The results of the forelimb grip strength test also indicated that the grip strength in the Model group was significantly lower than that in the Control group (p < 0.0001; Fig. 1J). Anatomical observations revealed that rats in the Model group exhibited a marked reduction in both the macroscopic appearance and cross-sectional area of their calf muscles, while no significant macroscopic morphological changes were observed in the femur (Fig. 1K–L). These findings collectively indicate a pronounced decrease in muscle mass and strength in the Model group rats, confirming the successful establishment of the OS model. The combination of reduced BMD (by DXA and µCT), decreased grip strength, and lower SMI in our DEX + OVX rats mirrors the clinical definition of osteosarcopenia proposed by the International Working Group on Osteosarcopenia (IWGO), supporting the translational relevance of this model.ICA intervention significantly reversed the aforementioned bone loss. Compared with the Model group, systemic and femoral bone mineral density significantly increased in the LD, MD, and HD groups (p < 0.01; Fig. 2A–B). Bone mineral salt content significantly increased in all LD, MD, and HD groups (p < 0.05), with a more pronounced trend in the LD group (p < 0.01; Fig. 2C). Bone mineral content showed no significant difference between LD and MD groups (p > 0.05) but significantly increased in the HD group (p < 0.001; Fig. 2D). Simultaneously, both fat content and FAT levels decreased significantly in all ICA intervention groups (p < 0.05), with the reduction exhibiting a dose-dependent increase (Fig. 2E–F). Micro-CT three-dimensional reconstruction and parametric analysis revealed (Fig. 2G-O) that compared with the Control group, the Model group exhibited severe impairment of microarchitecture in the distal femoral cancellous bone. This was manifested by significant reductions in bone volume (BV), tissue volume (TV), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular bone thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular bone number (Tb.N), connectivity density (Conn.D), and bone mineral density (BMD) were significantly reduced (p < 0.01), while trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) was markedly increased (p < 0.0001), fully confirming osteoporotic-like changes. ICA intervention, particularly at the high dose (HD), significantly improved all impaired bone microstructural parameters. Compared with the Model group, BV, TV, BV/TV, Tb.Th, Tb.N, Conn.D, and BMD were significantly increased (p < 0.01), while Tb.Sp was significantly decreased (p < 0.0001). This indicates that ICA effectively restores bone mass and improves bone microstructure, with the high dose demonstrating more pronounced effects.

[0058] [RESULTS] ICA effectively reverses muscle atrophy and improves tissue morphology

[0059] [RESULTS] DXA body composition analysis revealed that compared with the Control group, rats in the Model group exhibited significantly reduced total body muscle mass and SMI (p < 0.0001), indicating marked systemic muscle loss in the model group. Following ICA intervention, Total body muscle mass and SMI in the LD, MD, and HD groups significantly increased compared with the Model group (p < 0.01), demonstrating that ICA treatment was associated with promoting muscle mass recovery (Fig. 3A, B). HE staining revealed that in the Control group, muscle fibers in the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles exhibited regular arrangement, compact structure, and regular polygonal cross-sections. In contrast, the Model group showed marked muscle fiber atrophy, significantly reduced cross-sectional area (CSA), irregular fiber sizes, loose and disordered arrangement, accompanied by worsening interstitial fibrosis and fatty infiltration. Following ICA treatment, muscle fiber CSA in the LD, MD, and HD groups increased significantly compared to the Model group. Fiber arrangement became more orderly, and both fibrosis and fat infiltration were markedly reduced (Fig. 3C). The aforementioned findings were validated by quantitative examination of the cross-sectional area of muscle fibers using ImageJ software. The model group’s muscle fiber cross-sectional area was considerably smaller than that of the control group (p < 0.05). ICA intervention significantly improved muscle fiber cross-sectional area in both the medium-dose and high-dose groups (p < 0.05) when compared to the model group, showing a dose-dependent improvement trend (Fig. 3D). The results above indicate that ICA treatment is associated with significantly improved muscle pathological morphological abnormalities caused by OS.

[0061] [RESULTS] ICA modulates serum myostatin, inflammatory factors, and bone-muscle interaction factors while promoting the expression of key osteoblast proteins for musculoskeletal coupling

[0062] [RESULTS] ELISA detection of serum biomarkers revealed (Fig. 4A-D) that serum GDF-8 levels were significantly elevated in the Model group compared to the Control group (p < 0.0001), indicating enhanced muscle protein catabolism and a state of atrophy. Simultaneously, levels of CRP and IL-6 were significantly elevated in the Model group (p < 0.0001), indicating the presence of systemic inflammatory responses. Furthermore, serum FNDC5 concentrations in the Model group were significantly lower than those in the Control group (p < 0.0001), suggesting suppression of its signaling pathway. This may further exacerbate muscle breakdown and inflammatory states, reflecting impaired skeletal muscle-bone communication. ICA intervention significantly reversed these abnormalities: Compared with the Model group, serum GDF-8, CRP, and IL-6 levels were significantly reduced in all ICA dose groups (LD, MD, HD) (p < 0.0001), suggesting that ICA is associated with reducing systemic inflammation and inhibiting muscle deterioration. Simultaneously, serum FNDC5 concentrations significantly increased (p < 0.0001) in a dose-dependent manner, with the HD group exhibiting the most pronounced effect. This indicates that FNDC5 expression is increased in conjunction with ICA intervention, and that disrupted musculoskeletal signaling pathways may be repaired. To further investigate whether ICA influences bone metabolism through muscle-bone crosstalk, we examined the expression of osteogenesis-related proteins in muscle tissue. Western blot analysis revealed that compared to the Control group, the Model group exhibited significantly reduced expression of the key osteogenic transcription factors Runx2 and OCN in both the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles (p < 0.0001); Following ICA intervention, expression of both proteins was significantly upregulated (p < 0.05), suggesting a potential association between ICA treatment and promoting osteogenic activity via muscle-derived signaling (e.g., FNDC5/Irisin) (Fig. 4E-G).

[0064] [RESULTS] ICA inhibits pathological mitochondrial autophagy in muscle tissue and upregulates FNDC5 expression

[0065] [RESULTS] Western blot analysis was performed to examine the expression of key proteins in gastrocnemius and soleus muscle tissues (Fig. 5A-D). Results revealed that compared to the Control group, the Model group exhibited significantly elevated expression of PINK1, a key mitochondrial autophagy protein, and Parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, in muscle tissues (p < 0.0001). This indicates activation of the pathological mitochondrial autophagy pathway. Compared to the Model group, ICA intervention was associated with significantly suppressed abnormal overexpression of PINK1 and Parkin proteins across all dose groups (LD, MD, HD) (p < 0.0001), showing that the suppression of excessive mitochondrial autophagy in muscle tissue is correlated with ICA intervention. On the other hand, ICA treatment significantly increased FNDC5 protein expression (p < 0.0001) with a dose-dependent enhancement effect. Notably, a significant negative correlation was observed between PINK1/Parkin downregulation and FNDC5 upregulation, suggesting that ICA may restore FNDC5 signaling in association with inhibiting excessive mitochondrial autophagy. [0067] [RESULTS] ICA antagonizes CCCP-induced mitochondrial damage in C2C12 myotubes by protecting mitochondrial function and regulating the autophagy pathway

[0068] [RESULTS] This study successfully induced mitochondrial dysfunction in C2C12 myotubes through CCCP treatment, activating the PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitochondrial autophagy pathway. Mitochondrial membrane potential assessment revealed that the JC-1 aggregate/monomer fluorescence ratio in the C2C12 + CCCP group was significantly lower than that in the C2C12 group (p < 0.05), indicating depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane potential and severe functional impairment (Fig. 6A, B). Western blot and qPCR results (Fig. 6C–K) further revealed that both PINK1 and Parkin expression were significantly upregulated at the protein and mRNA levels following CCCP treatment (p < 0.01); In contrast to the typical pattern of LC3-II accumulation seen in many studies, the autophagosome marker protein LC3-II was considerably decreased (p < 0.05). The autophagosome marker protein LC3 was significantly reduced (p < 0.05), and the autophagy substrate protein p62 was also significantly downregulated (p < 0.01), while its mRNA levels showed no statistically significant changes. These findings indicate that CCCP alters the expression of autophagy-related proteins in a manner consistent with activation of the PINK1/Parkin pathway, although direct measurement of autophagic flux is required to confirm dynamic changes. Pre-treatment with ICA (300 µM) effectively antagonized the damaging effects of CCCP. Mitochondrial membrane potential measurements revealed that the red/green fluorescence ratio in the C2C12 + CCCP + ICA group was significantly elevated compared to the C2C12 + CCCP group (p < 0.05), yet remained lower than that in the C2C12 group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6A, B), indicating that ICA markedly mitigated CCCP-induced mitochondrial membrane potential collapse. Western blot and qPCR analyses revealed that ICA pretreatment significantly reduced PINK1 and Parkin expression at both the protein and mRNA levels (p < 0.05) compared to the CCCP group, while simultaneously inducing accumulation of LC3 and p62 proteins (p < 0.05). No significant changes were observed in the mRNA expression of either protein. These results indicate that ICA effectively suppressed CCCP-triggered mitochondrial autophagy initiation. More importantly, ICA intervention was associated with impaired autophagy flux, manifested by the accumulation of autophagy marker protein LC3 and substrate protein p62, suggesting that autophagosome clearance or degradation functions may be inhibited. Since no significant changes were observed in their mRNA levels, this accumulation effect is more likely to occur at the post-translational or protein degradation levels. Autophagy flow blockage may be associated with ICA inhibiting autophagy initiation or disrupting autophagosome maturation, transport, and fusion with lysosomes. The accumulation of p62 protein without corresponding mRNA changes provides key evidence for impaired autophagy flow.

This discovery provides new insights contributing to the understanding of the molecular mechanism of ICA.

[0073] [RESULTS] Diagnostic value of elevated FNDC5 levels for OS and genetic evidence for its protective causality

[0074] [RESULTS] To investigate the causal relationship between FNDC5 and OS, this study employed a combined strategy of SMR analysis and co-localization analysis. SMR utilizes genetic variation as an instrumental variable to assess the causal effects of FNDC5 expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) on OS-related phenotypes. Further co-localization analysis was conducted to examine whether eQTLs and OS share the same disease-causing genetic variants within the same genomic region. To enhance the robustness of causal inference, sensitivity analyses were performed. Leave-one-out analysis revealed that excluding any single SNP resulted in only minor changes in the overall error range of the estimated results, indicating that no single SNP was driving the overall estimate (Supplementary Materials Figure S3). Specifically, genetically predicted higher FNDC5 levels were positively associated with the following indicators: usual walking speed (OR = 1.016, 95% CI: 1.010–1.023, p < 0.001), left handgrip strength (OR = 1.014, 95% CI: 1.009–1.019, p < 0.001), femoral neck bone mineral density (OR = 1.064, 95% CI: 1.037–1.092, p < 0.001), forearm bone mineral density (OR = 1.392, 95% CI: 1.322–1.466, p < 0.001), and lumbar spine bone mineral density (OR = 1.056, 95% CI: 1.025–1.089, p < 0.001); while showing a negative correlation with calcaneal bone mineral density (OR = 0.998, 95% CI: 0.982–0.994, p < 0.001) (Fig. 8). In the co-localization analysis of eQTL and GWAS data, the posterior probability (SNP.PP.H4) consistently exceeded 0.95 (Fig. 9), further supporting the existence of shared causal genetic variation between FNDC5 and OS risk-associated phenotypes. Collectively, this study provides robust genetic evidence for the protective causal effect of FNDC5 on OS.

[0077] [DISCUSS] Discussion

[0078] [DISCUSS] This work offers supportive genetic evidence for this target and suggests, for the first time via SMR analysis, a potential protective causal link between FNDC5 and OS. These results are consistent with FNDC5 acting as a potential regulator of OS and a possible therapeutic target, and they are complementary to the ICA intervention phenotype shown in our research. More importantly, ICA effectively reverses the musculoskeletal metabolic coupling imbalance in OS models, and this reversal is associated with modulation of the “PINK1/Parkin–FNDC5” pathway. Combined with the supportive genetic evidence provided by SMR, this broadens our knowledge of OS pathogenesis and offers a theoretical and experimental basis for intervention strategies that target this route.

[0079] [DISCUSS] The DEX-OVX model of female rat OS effectively reproduces the key pathological characteristics of clinical OS Importantly, ICA markedly increased the expression of FNDC5 in both serum and muscle tissue, suggesting its potential involvement in metabolic control via the muscle-bone communication.

[0080] [DISCUSS] The findings indicate that the precise control of the skeletal muscle mitochondrial autophagy pathway is intimately linked to the musculoskeletal protective effects of ICA. The balance of mitochondrial autophagy, a critical process for mitochondrial quality maintenance, is essential for preserving cellular energy homeostasis. Autophagy at physiological levels aids in protecting cells and removing damaged mitochondria. However, excessive or sustained autophagy activation can lead to excessive mitochondrial clearance, impaired ATP synthesis, and exacerbated oxidative stress, driving cellular dysfunction and even death ​​. This study shows that after CCCP treatment, the expression of the autophagy substrate protein p62 was significantly downregulated, the autophagosome marker protein LC3 was significantly decreased, and the mitochondrial autophagy-related proteins and genes PINK1 and Parkin were significantly upregulated. These results suggest that the PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitochondrial autophagy pathway is activated by CCCP therapy. The significant decrease in p62 protein levels indicates that downstream autophagy activities, including autophagosome-lysosome fusion and contents destruction, are improved concurrently. The concurrent drop in LC3-II in our experimental setting, which deviates from the typical pattern of LC3-II accumulation, may be regarded as a possible sign of altered autophagic flux. However, in the absence of direct autophagic flow studies (e.g., employing bafilomycin A1), this conclusion is still theoretical. Overall, our findings imply that autophagic degradation may have been increased, and mitochondrial autophagy was activated. PINK1 and Parkin expression, on the other hand, was markedly downregulated after ICA intervention, which restored mitochondrial membrane potential and encouraged the accumulation of p62 and LC3 proteins. Interestingly, ICA did not totally block basal autophagy levels; rather, it only appeared to inhibit excessive autophagy under pathogenic settings. This “selective regulation” characteristic is essential because it prevents the buildup of metabolic waste that could arise from over-inhibition of basal autophagy while safeguarding mitochondrial function and muscle balance. ICA dramatically inhibited the start of mitochondrial autophagy brought on by CCCP. More significantly, ICA was associated with the autophagy flow being blocked. This was primarily reflected in LC3 protein accumulation, increased autophagosome numbers, or reduced clearance. Significant p62 protein buildup suggests compromised autophagic degradation. The suppression of mitochondrial autophagy starts with ICA, and possible disruption of autophagosome maturation, transport, or fusion/degradation with lysosomes may be linked to this autophagy flow restriction. The main indicator of compromised autophagy flow is the buildup of p62 protein in the absence of statistically significant mRNA alterations. This is consistent with abnormalities in mitochondrial homeostasis found in research on diabetic sarcopenia and disuse atrophy. One of the main pathogenic processes causing sarcopenia and subsequent bone loss is excessive mitochondrial autophagy.

[0081] [DISCUSS] Notably, the CCCP-induced in vitro mitophagy model successfully replicates the central pathogenic nexus of “excessive PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy coupled with FNDC5 downregulation” seen in vivo, although differing from the complex systemic DEX + OVX model in its induction process. This convergence supports the analysis of this particular chemical axis using the CCCP model. The suggestion that ICA ameliorates OS by focusing on this common channel is reinforced by the complementary results from both models.

[0082] [DISCUSS] Myokine has a beneficial effect on satellite cell development and skeletal muscle growth. It also regulates metabolic activity in the bones, liver, heart, and other organs via endocrine pathways. The main muscle factors that regulate bone metabolism that have been widely reported in recent years include interleukin-6 (IL-6) 、Irisin 、β-amino isobutyric acid (L-BAIBA) 、myostatin, and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). The transmembrane protein FNDC5 is mostly expressed in skeletal muscle. Its extracellular component is broken down by proteolysis, releasing the active peptide Irisin into the circulation. Irisin is highly sensitive to skeletal muscle and skeletal diseases. Scropenia, osteoporosis, and osteosarcopenia are all thought to be predicted by it. On the other hand, FNDC5 is an upstream component that is essential for preserving bone mass. It can, however, counteract the muscular atrophy factor GDF-8 and prevent muscle catabolism. According to this study, the main way through which ICA may enhance OS is through the “PINK1/Parkin–FNDC5” route. Our findings suggest that ICA is associated with reversing the decrease in FNDC5 expression and restoring the myoskeletal signaling exchange mediated by FNDC5, potentially via blocking pathological mitochondrial autophagy. By demonstrating that pharmacological inhibition of the PINK1/Parkin pathway is sufficient to upregulate FNDC5 and that ICA may act through this axis, the inhibitor experiment further supports a potential causal relationship. The study also found that FNDC5 was significantly downregulated in both in vitro and in vivo models, along with upregulation of the autophagy marker PINK1/Parkin and mitochondrial malfunction. As a key “myokine” released by skeletal muscle, FNDC5/Irisin has been shown to have a number of advantageous effects, such as enhancing metabolism, preventing bone resorption, encouraging bone growth, and having anti-inflammatory qualities. Important osteogenic proteins like Runx2 and OCN were found to be increased in muscle tissue after ICA treatment. This implies that ICA may reverse metabolic decoupling in OS by indirectly promoting bone formation and improving microarchitecture through the muscle-bone axis when combined with higher blood FNDC5 levels. While our data show a strong correlation between inhibition of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy and upregulation of FNDC5, the exact molecular interplay remains unclear. It is possible that improved mitochondrial function secondarily enhances FNDC5 expression, perhaps through AMPK/PGC-1α signaling, a well-known pathway for irisin regulation. Future studies should investigate whether AMPK is involved in ICA’s effects.

[0083] [DISCUSS] The results of this work have important scientific and practical ramifications: using SMR analysis, we provided genetic causal evidence for the first time that the “PINK1/Parkin–FNDC5” pathway is closely associated with musculoskeletal-linked metabolism. Our correlative data indicate that targeting abnormal mitochondrial autophagy in skeletal muscle and restoring FNDC5 signaling may be a promising new treatment approach for OS. Our results show that the musculoskeletal protective effects of ICA are associated with the regulation of skeletal muscle mitochondrial autophagy. Being a natural product, ICA is a very interesting candidate medication or functional ingredient because of its bidirectional regulatory qualities that target both muscle and bone.

[0084] [DISCUSS] This study’s limitations include the following: (1) This study focuses primarily on mitochondrial autophagy in muscle tissue. The effects of ICA on bone metabolism and osteoblast or osteocyte mitochondrial function are still unclear. (2) Further in vivo confirmation using genetic techniques (such as gene silencing) is required despite the compelling evidence of an association between FNDC5 and mitochondrial autophagy. (3) The DEX + OVX model used in this study mostly mimics postmenopausal women’s osteoporosis (OS). Extending its findings to OS brought on by other etiologies (such as aging, men, or disuse) calls for caution and additional model validation. (4) This study used skeletal muscle-derived FNDC5 eQTL data in SMR analysis to improve biological relevance. Nevertheless, even when using tissue-specific eQTLs, the inferred relationships mostly reflect statistical robustness due to the cross-tissue characteristics of GWAS pooled data. Further functional tests are needed to clarify the exact tissue- and cell-specific processes underlying these relationships. (5) Our finding of reduced LC3 in conjunction with p62 degradation offers a pattern that departs from the traditional autophagic flux marker response in the interpretation of in vitro CCCP-induced mitophagy. In the absence of direct flux measurement with lysosomal inhibitors (e.g., bafilomycin A1), conclusions about the specific dynamics of autophagic flow after ICA intervention and CCCP treatment in this model should be regarded as preliminary and require further experimental validation. (6) The icariin doses used in this study, particularly 120 mg/kg, are substantially higher than those tested in human trials. Without measurement of plasma concentrations or assessment of long-term safety, the clinical feasibility of such doses remains speculative. Future studies should include pharmacokinetic analysis and dose-ranging studies to better inform translation. (7) Regarding bone microarchitecture, additional parameters such as trabecular pattern factor (Tb.Pf) were not calculated in this study; future work should include more comprehensive microarchitectural parameters.

[0085] [CONCL] Conclusion

[0086] [CONCL] This study indicates that modulation of the PINK1/Parkin–FNDC5 pathway is closely associated with the therapeutic effects of ICA in this postmenopausal DEX-OVX-induced OS model, warranting future investigation using genetic approaches. ICA restores homeostasis in the muscle-bone metabolic coupling in association with inhibition of hyperactivated mitochondrial autophagy and upregulation of FNDC5. SMR analysis provides genetic-level evidence supporting the protective causal role of FNDC5 in OS. This finding establishes the theoretical basis for the “mitochondrial quality control–myofibrillar factor axis” in skeletal muscle-bone co-disease.
